I posted [Who still remembers this HTML tag?](https://www.reddit.com/r/webdev/comments/1syrvjj/who_still_remembers_this_html_tag/) on r/webdev as a dumb screenshot and walked away smug. Then the replies rolled in and suddenly I was fifteen again: nested marquees, someone calling `<blink>` the “partner in crime,” `<center>` holding up entire pages, and enough GeoCities callbacks that I could smell the free web host through the screen.

Some people were delighted. Some said the combo gave them headaches. Both reactions felt honest. What I kept thinking about afterwards was simpler: a ton of that markup **still parses and sometimes still moves in a real browser**, even though every doc I trust tells us not to build new work on it. So this post is my attempt to pin down what I think I know: how it looked in the wild, what I remember feeling, and what the specs politely call it now.

I am writing it for Future Me too, and for anyone who got pulled into that thread at 11 p.m. and suddenly remembered the exact sound of a dial-up handshake. Tiny samples of the actual markup, links when I want receipts, and a playground because I still cannot leave a `<marquee>` alone. Treat every snippet like a fossil: great campfire story, terrible dependency for a launch.

## The marquee era

I will be honest: `<marquee>` was my first idea of “animation.” No keyframes, no easing curves, just the promise that text could *go somewhere* because the browser said so. Personal pages, “under construction” banners, and that portfolio where someone tried to paint clouds with pure manic energy all leaned on it. Technically it shoves text or inline content along an axis: scroll, slide, bounce, repeat.

```html
<marquee direction="left" behavior="scroll" scrollamount="6">
  ★ Welcome to my home page !!! ★
</marquee>
```

When I put the spec hat on, the picture is blunt: `<marquee>` is **obsolete** in the HTML sense. Engines may still parse and render it for crusty legacy pages, but I would not bet a client checkout flow on it. Emotionally it is also **rough on readers**. I get distracted by looping motion even when I am the one who turned it on; for people with [vestibular or motion sensitivity](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/@media/prefers-reduced-motion) it is worse than annoying. That is why the toy below bails out when the OS asks for reduced motion.

When I want motion today, I reach for **CSS animations or transitions**, keep **`prefers-reduced-motion`** in the back pocket, and leave the “DVD logo corner bounce” trick (nested marquees, different directions) in a CodePen tab where it cannot hurt anyone’s stakeholders.

## Blink: the Netscape strobe

Someone in that thread called `<blink>` marquee’s accomplice, and I laughed because it hurt. `<blink>` toggled text on and off like it owed you money. Growing up on a machine that actually honored it felt like wielding forbidden power; opening the same page somewhere else and seeing static text was my first peek behind the curtain.

It was a **Netscape thing**, not a stable cross-browser standard the way I teach HTML now. Internet Explorer skipped it; other engines chased different shiny objects (`<marquee>` drama on their side). That mismatch quietly taught me what a **support matrix** was before I learned the vocabulary.

```html
<p>
  <blink>PARTY LIKE IT'S 1996</blink>
</p>
```

You will not see that flash in most browsers today, which makes this sample feel like a museum placard. `<blink>` is a punchline because it literally fights readability and accessibility standards. I am not reviving it, and if you are tempted for the bit: typography and color hierarchy still win every time.

## Presentational HTML: `<center>`, `<font>`, and friends

I distinctly remember the phase where “layout” meant right-click, View Source, and a blush-inducing wall of `<font>` tags. Comic Sans was not ironic yet. Before CSS really won, I styled pages by sprinkling presentational HTML everywhere; nobody handed me a design system, so I hoarded inline attributes like treasure.

```html
<center>
  <font face="Comic Sans MS" size="5" color="#ff00ff">
    Welcome 2 my page!!!
  </font>
</center>
```

- **`<center>`** horizontally centered its contents. The spec-minded read: it is **obsolete** in HTML5-style documents. I use CSS instead (`margin: auto`, flexbox, grid, `text-align`).
- **`<font>`** set face, size, and color inline. Also **obsolete** for new documents. My brain now reaches for `font-family`, `font-size`, `color`, and whatever design tokens the project already has.

They still exist in the parser for **legacy markup** and I am grateful for that when I open some ancient saved HTML. I still would not ship them on something new.

## Frames: whole pages were jigsaw puzzles

**`<frameset>`** and **`<frame>`** chopped the viewport into panes, each loading its own document. I can still picture the tiny templates: `header.html`, `nav.html`, `content.html`, like a static-site generator built out of guilt. Hit counters and sticky nav columns lived in their own little worlds. It looked modular when I sketched it on paper. In real life it shredded **bookmarking, deep linking, and focus** for anyone who just wanted to share one URL and land in the right place.

```html
<frameset rows="80,*">
  <frame src="header.html" />
  <frameset cols="200,*">
    <frame src="nav.html" />
    <frame src="main.html" name="content" />
  </frameset>
</frameset>
```

The framing model is **obsolete** for the kind of pages I build today. When I embed another document, **`<iframe>`** is the tool, with tight sizing, sandboxing, and the guilty performance audit when I own both sides.

## Tables for layout (the trope)

Confession: I absolutely laid out whole sites with `<table>`, `<tr>`, and `<td>` because tutorials said “put it in a table” and CSS layout felt like guesswork with bruises. I could *see* the grid in my head; the markup was lying, but it worked until it did not. That habit is **not** the same as `<table>` going away. **Tables are still correct for tabular data.** The sin was **layout abuse**: wrenching table markup until it became a grid you never admitted out loud.

```html
<table width="100%" cellpadding="8" border="0">
  <tr>
    <td colspan="2" bgcolor="#000080"><font color="#ffffff">~~ My Site ~~</font></td>
  </tr>
  <tr>
    <td width="160" valign="top">Links…</td>
    <td valign="top">Main content here (and a nested table if you were brave)</td>
  </tr>
</table>
```

These days, after enough table-shaped scar tissue, I lean on **semantic landmarks**, **flexbox**, **grid**, and **logical properties** for layout. I save tables for what they were meant for: data in rows and columns, not for smuggling a homepage into a spreadsheet costume.

## `<strong>` vs `<b>`, `<em>` vs `<i>` (they are not “the old bad tags”)

That Reddit rabbit hole resurfaced a question I used to feel guilty about: are `<b>` and `<i>` deprecated? **They are not.** They are still valid elements with defined meaning in HTML. **`<b>`** and **`<i>`** can mark spans where you want **bold** or **italic** without necessarily implying extra semantic “importance” or “stress emphasis”; **`<strong>`** and **`<em>`** carry **strong importance** and **emphasis** semantics.

```html
<p>
  <strong>Warning:</strong> this is important to the sentence meaning.
  <b>BrandName</b> might be bold for style, not louder emphasis.
</p>
<p>
  <em>This</em> is stressed wording; <i>Homo sapiens</i> might be italic for a Latin name.
</p>
```

For me the shift was learning that “bold” is not automatically “shouting,” and that the honest question is *why* the text looks different. That is mostly **documentation and culture**, not some mass funeral for tags that offended the lint rules in my head for a few years.

## Beyond tags: the texture of the old web

The tags were loud, but the *culture* was louder. I still smile, and sometimes cringe, when I remember:

- **Under construction** GIFs that promised a million-dollar redesign someday, paired with “best viewed in Netscape” badges like we were handing out varsity letters.
- **Visitor counters** fed by `/cgi-bin/` scripts, because your page had not truly existed until a number ticked upward in public.
- **JavaScript image rollovers** before `:hover` was a comfort zone, which meant every nav bar carried a faint smell of `onmouseover` duct tape.
- **Flash and Shockwave**, their own universe of motion and sound, later kneecapped by open standards, phones that refused to melt, and the slow public argument about **battery, security, and accessibility** (Steve Jobs weighing in is forever burned into my RSS memory).

None of that replaces good HTML, yet it explains why a single `<marquee>` meme can still light up a comment section. The web I grew up on was **loud** on purpose, and I kind of miss the noise even when I refuse to ship it.

## Nostalgia versus the reader

I love remembering this stuff; it reminds me why I care about the web. I do not love subjecting real people to it on a deadline.

Standards, for me, are the compromise that lets us be weird in the chat thread and disciplined in production. The early web showed what not to repeat. The modern stack gives me better tools to say the same thing: **clearly, accessibly, and maintainably**.

If you are still babysitting a legacy page that marquees: give it a real heading, a calm sentence of copy, and a CSS animation that honors `prefers-reduced-motion`. Your future self and your readers will quietly thank you, and you will sleep better than I did the night I nested three marquees “just to see.”
